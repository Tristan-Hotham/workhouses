What's left of the workhouse?

For a century the workhouse was the last resort of the poor in Britain and Ireland. Most have gone, but they left traces: buildings that became hospitals and flats, lanes and hamlets named after them, and in Ireland the burial grounds of the famine. This is a map of those traces in open data, and an honest account of how much is missing.

Most market towns in Britain and Ireland once had a workhouse, a large and deliberately forbidding building where the destitute were given food and shelter in return for work and their liberty. The New Poor Law of 1834 built them across England and Wales, the Irish Poor Law of 1838 put one in every Irish union, and Scotland built its poorhouses after 1845. They were abolished in stages between 1930 and 1948, but the buildings and the memories did not vanish.

There is no open, complete list of them. The authority on the subject, Peter Higginbotham's workhouses.org.uk, is a lifetime of research that is not open data, and I have not used it as a source. So this study asks a different question: what traces of the workhouse can be found in the open record? I searched four sources: the listed building registers of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, OpenStreetMap, Wikidata and Wikipedia. I found {{traces.n|int}} traces and sorted them into five kinds: surviving buildings, sites and memorials where the building has gone, burial grounds, street and place names such as Workhouse Lane, and businesses that use the name.

    02 · A short historyFrom the parish poorhouse to the union workhouse

    The history below comes from the literature, not from the data. The poor law began with the parish. Every parish had to look after its own poor, mostly by giving them money or food at home. Workhouses came later, first parish by parish and then, after 1834, as a national system built on the idea that relief inside them should be harsh enough to deter all but the truly destitute.

    1. 1601The Act for the Relief of the Poor makes each parish responsible for its poor, paid for by a local rate and run by overseers. Most relief is given at home. Slack (1990)
    2. 1723Knatchbull's Workhouse Test Act lets parishes, alone or together, set up workhouses and refuse relief to anyone who will not enter one. Small parish workhouses and poorhouses spread across England. Slack (1990); Higginbotham (2012)
    3. 1834The Poor Law Amendment Act groups parishes into unions run by elected boards of guardians under a central commission. Relief for the able-bodied is to be given in the workhouse, on terms worse than the poorest independent labourer's. Around {{context.ew_unions|int}} unions build or adapt workhouses over the following decades. Crowther (1981); Morrison (1999)
    4. 1838The Poor Relief (Ireland) Act divides Ireland into {{context.ie_unions_1838|int}} unions, each with a workhouse to a standard design by George Wilkinson. Most open between 1840 and the mid-1840s, just as the Great Famine begins; by the early 1850s there are {{context.ie_unions_1850s|int}} unions. O'Connor (1995); Kinealy (1994)
    5. 1845The Poor Law Amendment (Scotland) Act sets up parochial boards, which build poorhouses, often shared between several parishes as combination poorhouses. Higginbotham (2012)
    6. 1930The Local Government Act 1929 abolishes the boards of guardians in England and Wales from 1 April 1930. Workhouses become public assistance institutions, and many of their infirmaries become hospitals. In the Irish Free State the workhouses had already given way to county homes and hospitals in the 1920s. Crowther (1981); O'Connor (1995)
    7. 1948The National Assistance Act ends the poor law in Great Britain, and Northern Ireland ends its own in the same year. Many former workhouses pass to the new National Health Service as hospitals. Crowther (1981); Fowler (2007)

    03 · What survivesMostly buildings in Britain, mostly sites and graves in Ireland

    Grouping the traces that lie within a few hundred metres of each other gives {{sites.n|int}} workhouse sites: {{sites.by_status.building|int}} with at least one surviving building in the record, {{sites.by_status.site|int}} known only as a site, plaque or memorial, and {{sites.by_status.burial|int}} known only from a burial ground. {{sites.with_listed|int}} sites include a listed building. Of the workhouse list entries, {{listed.n|int}} in all, nearly every one in England and Wales is Grade II ({{listed.england_wales_ii_pct|pc}}); the only Grade I is the poor house inside the walls of Framlingham Castle in Suffolk, built in {{listed.framlingham_built|raw}} and listed with the castle.

    Workhouse sites by nation and what is left

    {{sites.n|int}} sites built from {{traces.placed|int}} mapped traces (buildings, sites and burial grounds within 350 m of each other count as one site).

    The list entries also show how old and small many surviving workhouses are. In England and Wales {{listed.ew_named_poorhouse|int}} entries call themselves a poor house, poorhouse or parish workhouse and {{listed.ew_old_workhouse|int}} are simply "The Old Workhouse", against {{listed.ew_named_union|int}} that name a union, a hundred or a poor law institution. Many of the cottages and houses on the list are the small parish workhouses of the eighteenth century, the ones the 1834 Act replaced, rather than the great union workhouses that replaced them.

    04 · What they becameHospitals, homes and a handful of museums

    For {{reuse.known|int}} of the {{reuse.n_building_sites|int}} sites with a surviving building ({{reuse.known_pct|pc}}), the names, descriptions or map tags say what the building is now or became. The two biggest groups are hospitals ({{reuse.by.hospital|int}}) and homes ({{reuse.by.homes|int}}), between them {{reuse.hosp_homes_pct|pc}} of those with a known use. That fits the history. Workhouse infirmaries became municipal hospitals after 1930 and passed to the National Health Service in 1948, and when those hospitals closed many of the buildings were sold and turned into flats.

    What surviving workhouses became

    {{reuse.known|int}} sites with a surviving building whose later use is recorded. 'Homes' includes buildings now known by a house name. Use not stated for {{reuse.unknown|int}} more.

    {{reuse.by.museum|int}} have become museums or heritage sites, from the National Trust's workhouse at Southwell in Nottinghamshire, often called the best preserved in England, to Gressenhall in Norfolk, Llanfyllin in Powys, Ripon in North Yorkshire and the Cinema Museum in the old Lambeth workhouse, where Charlie Chaplin was briefly an inmate as a child. In Ireland, Donaghmore in County Laois and Dunfanaghy in Donegal tell the story of the famine. Others are offices or community buildings ({{reuse.by.offices|int}}), a clothing factory, a studio and a crafts school, and {{reuse.by.ruin|int}} are ruins.

    05 · Names on the mapWorkhouse Lane, Workhouse Green and Workhouse End

    When the building has gone, the name often stays. OpenStreetMap and the lists hold {{names.n|int}} streets, places and features named after a workhouse or poorhouse: {{names.lanes|int}} Workhouse or Poor House Lanes, and roads, hills, farms, woods, bridges, a brook, a coppice and a cycleway. Hamlets keep the name too: Workhouse Green in Suffolk and South Yorkshire, Workhouse End in Bedfordshire and Lincolnshire. {{names.england_pct|pc}} of these names are in England, and they cluster in the east and south: Essex and Norfolk have {{names.top_counties.0.n|int}} each and Hampshire {{names.top_counties.2.n|int}}.

    What the names are attached to

    {{names.n|int}} street, place and feature names containing workhouse, poorhouse or poor house (each street counted once).

    Most of these lanes are rural. Many probably led to an early parish workhouse, or to land owned by one, rather than to a later union workhouse in town. Then there are {{business.n|int}} businesses that use the name: gyms, cafés, a hairdresser, a campsite. Most are puns on work, but {{business.at_site|word}} are at or beside a workhouse site: the campsite at Llanfyllin, and a coffee shop next to the old Reading workhouse, later Battle Hospital.

    06 · IrelandThe famine workhouse

    Ireland's traces look different. Of the {{sites.ie.n|int}} workhouse sites on the island, {{sites.ie.building_pct|pc}} have a surviving building in the record, against {{sites.gb.building_pct|pc}} in Britain. The rest are known as sites, memorials or burial grounds. Part of that gap is about the data: the Republic's own register of historic buildings is not among my sources, so no Irish building outside Northern Ireland reaches the map through a listing. But the burial grounds are not about the data in the same way. Of the {{ireland.burial_all|int}} workhouse burial grounds mapped in OpenStreetMap, {{ireland.burial_ie|int}} are in Ireland: workhouse graveyards in Donegal, Clare, Laois and Tipperary, a cillín at Bantry, and the workhouse cemeteries of Loughlinstown, Naas and Bawnboy. Britain's only one is the Bath Union Workhouse burial ground, with its unmarked graves.

    What is left: Britain and Ireland compared

    Share of workhouse sites by what survives in the record ({{sites.gb.n|int}} sites in Britain, {{sites.ie.n|int}} on the island of Ireland).

    The Irish workhouses opened just before the Great Famine of 1845 to 1852 and were overwhelmed by it. Many held far more people than they were built for, fever spread through them, and the dead were often buried in mass graves on or beside the site (Kinealy 1994; O'Connor 1995). One trace in the data says this more plainly than any summary. In 2005 the remains of {{ireland.kilkenny_n|int}} people were found at the site of the old Kilkenny workhouse. A memorial there now reads: "Herein lie the remains of {{ireland.kilkenny_n|int}} famine victims discovered at this Workhouse site in 2005. They were paupers of the Kilkenny Union Workhouse whose deaths occurred between {{ireland.kilkenny_from|raw}} and {{ireland.kilkenny_to|raw}}. Their names were not recorded."

    In Britain the workhouse survives as hospitals and flats. In Ireland it survives as graves.

    The sites without buildings are also a record of what mappers have chosen to map. {{ireland.site_of_osm|int}} workhouse sites in {{ireland.site_of_counties|int}} Irish counties, mostly in Leinster and Kerry, are mapped in OpenStreetMap as "site of" the union workhouse, which is a large part of why Meath, Dublin, Wicklow and Kerry stand out on the map. In Northern Ireland, where the listed building register is part of my data, all {{sites.nation_status.Northern Ireland.building|int}} sites have a surviving building, several of them still hospitals.

    07 · How complete is this?A map of the record, not of the workhouses

    Read this before reading the map

    This is not a complete list of workhouses, and it should not be used as one. There were around {{context.ew_unions|int}} poor law unions in England and Wales and {{context.ie_unions_1850s|int}} in Ireland by the 1850s, plus dozens of Scottish poorhouses and thousands of earlier parish workhouses. The open record finds {{complete.ew_sites|int}} sites in England and Wales, many of them small parish poorhouses, and {{complete.ie_sites|int}} on the island of Ireland, about {{complete.ie_pct_of_163|pc}} of the number of Irish unions.

    The sources barely overlap. {{sites.one_source_pct|pc}} of sites appear in only one of the four sources, which says how far each source is from complete. Listed buildings are found only when their list entry's name says workhouse or poorhouse, so a workhouse listed as "St Mary's Hospital" is missed. OpenStreetMap depends on what volunteers have mapped and tagged. Wikidata and Wikipedia cover the famous and the well documented. For the full story of any workhouse, go to Peter Higginbotham's workhouses.org.uk.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Listed buildings. Every entry on the four UK lists (Historic England's National Heritage List for England, Historic Environment Scotland, Cadw and Northern Ireland's Department for Communities), as harmonised in my study of listed buildings. I kept entries whose names include workhouse, work house, poorhouse, poor house, house of industry, union house, poor law, casual ward, board of guardians or vagrants, and left out bridewells and houses of correction (prisons), pauper lunatic asylums, the Norwich Union and Grand Union buildings and other false friends. Farms, bridges and yards named after a workhouse count as names.

    OpenStreetMap. {{raw.osm|int}} features in Britain and Ireland whose name contains workhouse, union house, poor house or house of industry. Each is classed from its tags: buildings, historic workhouses and museums as buildings; "site of" features, plaques and heritage trails as sites; graveyards and cemeteries as burial grounds; roads, places, farms and woods as names; cafés, gyms and shops as businesses. Electricity transformers, canal and insurance names and a townland whose English name only sounds like a workhouse are left out.

    Wikidata and Wikipedia. The {{raw.wd_gbie|int}} items in Britain and Ireland that Wikidata classes as a workhouse or poorhouse, and the {{raw.wp|int}} pages in English Wikipedia's workhouse categories for the UK and Ireland, leaving out poems, people, societies and almshouses. The other {{raw.wd_abroad|int}} Wikidata workhouses are abroad, most of them in Germany ({{raw.wd_abroad_top.0.n|int}}). {{traces.unplaced|int}} items have no coordinates and are listed under the map but not drawn.

    Duplicates and sites. The same building often appears in several sources. Traces that share a list entry or a Wikidata item, or have nearly the same name within 150 m, are merged; repeated street segments with the same name within 1.5 km count once. Buildings, sites and burial grounds within 350 m of each other form one site. What a building became is read from words in its name, description and tags (hospital, flats, museum, offices and so on), with {{method.n_ovr|word}} corrections by hand where the words mislead. Houses known only by a house name count as homes, which is a weaker kind of evidence.
